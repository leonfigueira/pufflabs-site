Biodiversity Net Gain, explained for people who are not ecologists

Developers in England now have to leave nature measurably better off. When they cannot manage that on their own site, they pay somebody else to do it on theirs. That somebody could be you. This guide walks through what the money really looks like, what it costs, what you sign up to for 30 years, and everything that can go wrong.

Read everything here against this

In August 2026 the government took Biodiversity Net Gain off developments of 0.2 hectares or less, and off temporary permissions. Defra's own estimate is that the 0.2 hectare change alone removes the requirement from about half of all residential planning permissions. A further exemption for brownfield housing was consulted on during 2026. The demand side of this market is shrinking, and the commitment you would be making runs for 30 years with no exit.

A note on units of measurement. Government measures land in hectares, so this guide does too. One hectare is about 2.5 acres. Ten hectares is about 25 acres. Sixty acres is about 24 hectares.

    Written for landowners, farmers, first time land buyers and anyone who has been pitched a Biodiversity Net Gain deal and wants to understand it before signing. It is information, not professional advice. Every number here is dated and linked to its source so you can check it yourself.

    The rule

    One sentence, then the detail

    Most larger new development in England must leave the site at least 10% better for wildlife than it found it, and keep it that way for 30 years.

    That is Biodiversity Net Gain, or BNG. It became a legal condition of planning permission for major development on 12 February 2024, and for small sites on 2 April 2024. It comes from the Environment Act 2021. Since August 2026 the smallest sites are exempt, and Defra thinks that alone takes out about half of residential planning permissions, which matters a great deal to anyone planning to sell units.

    Wildlife value is not judged by eye. It is counted, in biodiversity units, using a government spreadsheet called the statutory biodiversity metric. A developer measures the units on the land before, works out the units after, and has to end up 10% ahead.

    Why anybody would pay you for this

    A housing estate that covers its whole site in houses and roads cannot create enough habitat on that site to get 10% ahead. It has a shortfall. The law lets it make that shortfall up somewhere else, on somebody else's land, by buying units from them.

    Source: GOV.UK, Understanding biodiversity net gain. Checked 7 September 2026.

    Where the money comes from

    The developer has three options, in order

    The law makes a developer work down a hierarchy. Understanding it tells you exactly where your land sits in the queue.

    1. Do it on site

    Create or improve habitat inside the red line of their own development. Cheapest for them. Always tried first on major schemes.

    2. Buy off site units

    Pay a landowner who has registered a biodiversity gain site. This is you. This is the entire private market.

    3. Buy statutory credits

    Buy from the government as a last resort. Deliberately expensive so it does not undercut the private market.

    Those government credits matter to you even though you will never sell one, because they set a rough ceiling on what anyone will pay you. But read the ceiling correctly, because this is the single easiest number to get wrong.

    A statutory credit is worth half a unit

    GOV.UK: "One credit is worth 0.5 units." A developer must buy two credits for every one unit they are short. So the ceiling on a low or medium distinctiveness unit is not £42,000, it is £84,000.

    Credit prices run from £42,000 for low and most medium distinctiveness habitat, through £48,000 and £66,000, to £125,000 for high distinctiveness ponds, coastal habitat and most woodland, and £650,000 for high distinctiveness lakes. Hedgerows are £44,000 and watercourses £230,000. All of those are per credit, so double them for the per unit ceiling.

    One quirk worth knowing: traditional orchards sit in the cheapest tier despite being high distinctiveness.

    GOV.UK is unusually blunt about why the prices are set where they are: they include "a mark-up to prevent statutory credit prices undercutting the off-site market" and are set high so they do "not compete with the development of the private market". It also says plainly that "Statutory credit prices are not guideline prices for off-site biodiversity units." They are a ceiling, not a valuation.

    Sources: GOV.UK, Statutory biodiversity credit prices and Statutory biodiversity credits. Checked 7 September 2026.

    The shape of the deal

    What a landowner is actually selling

    You are not selling land. You are not selling a crop. You are selling a promise, backed by a legal agreement, that a piece of your land will become and stay better for wildlife for at least 30 years, and a share of the units that promise generates.

    The buyer needs those units to get planning permission. Once they are allocated to their development on the public register, they are used up. They cannot be sold twice.

    The two ways it is done

    Habitat banking. You create the habitat first and look for buyers afterwards. You carry all the risk, and there is a real mechanical reason you keep more of the value. The metric has a "habitat created in advance" function: every year the habitat has been growing before the units are sold gives back a year of the time discount. Create five years early and you get five years of that penalty back. This is the strongest argument for habitat banking and almost nobody explains it. The mirror image also exists, a delay function that penalises you year by year if you register and then stall.

    Bespoke creation. A specific developer agrees to buy first, and you create the habitat to suit them. Much less risk, usually less money, and you need a developer to find you.

    Source: Defra, BNG flowcharts for landowners (PDF).

    The pitch

    The version you will hear first

    It usually goes something like this. Buy a field for £70,000. Get it registered as a biodiversity gain site. A developer pays you enough on completion to cover what you paid for the land, and after that the units keep selling. Maybe a £300,000 lump sum. Or take it spread over 30 years instead.

    None of that is impossible. Deals in that shape exist. The arithmetic on a good site in the right place genuinely can look like that, and the next few pages show you exactly how the numbers are built so you can test any pitch against them.

    What the pitch leaves out

    It is a private market with no guaranteed buyer. GOV.UK's own guidance for land managers says it plainly: "Off-site BNG is a private market and there is no guarantee you will find a buyer."

    It is unregulated. There is no ombudsman, no compensation scheme, and no licensing of the brokers and agents selling these deals.

    And the money rarely arrives as one cheque

    Payment structures are negotiable and they vary a lot. In practice you will be offered one of:

    • A lump sum when units are allocated to a development. Cleanest, rarest, and usually the lowest total.
    • Staged payments released as the habitat hits agreed milestones. Common, and it means the money arrives across years while your costs start immediately.
    • Results based payments that only land if the habitat reaches its target condition.
    • Annual payments across the term, which is what most habitat bank operators offer.

    Whichever it is, the units only sell when a developer near you needs them. The next step shows how often that has actually happened.

    Source: GOV.UK, Sell biodiversity units as a land manager. Checked 7 September 2026.

    The reality check

    What the public register actually shows

    Every off site biodiversity gain site in England is on one public register run by Natural England, and so is every sale. Anyone can read it. The register had grown to 367 sites by 7 September 2026. Every figure and percentage below comes from a complete download of the register on 21 August 2026, when it held 350 sites, and the proportions were unchanged when we re-checked a sample on 7 September.

    350registered gain sites in England
    10,671hectares committed
    2,710allocations to developments, all time
    3,062area units allocated, all time

    Now the two numbers that matter most, and that no brochure will show you.

    The median site has sold 0.83 units

    Half of all registered sites in England have sold less than one unit in total. At a typical £25,000 a unit that is under £21,000 of gross revenue. Against that sits £15,000 or more of setup before a blade of grass is planted, and then 30 years of management and monitoring that on a five hectare site runs past £80,000. The median site is not marginally short. It is six figures down across the term.

    39% have sold nothing at all

    137 of the 350 sites had no units allocated to any development. Some of those registered recently and have simply not had time. Many did not.

    Meanwhile the best performing single site had sold 183 units. This is a market with a few large winners and a very long tail of sites that have sold almost nothing. Averages hide that. The average site had sold 8.7 units, ten times the median, because a handful of large sites pull the average up.

    The concentration is stark. The 25 best selling sites, out of 350, took half of every unit ever sold. The top 10 took nearly a third.

    78% of individual sales were for less than one unit. The typical transaction is a small builder buying a fraction of a unit, not a developer buying your whole field.

    The demand itself is spread widely. Developments in 307 different local planning authorities have bought units. So the problem is not that nobody is buying. It is that most sites are not where, or not what, the buying is, and that each authority absorbs very little. The median local planning authority has taken just 3.9 units in total since the register opened. Only 7 authorities out of 307 have ever absorbed more than 57 units.

    Two things the register does not do

    It records allocations to developments, not sales, and it publishes no prices at all. There is no public record of what anybody has actually paid for a biodiversity unit in England. Every price you will read anywhere, including in this guide, is an asking price or a survey of asking prices.

    Running the registered habitats through Defra's own multipliers gives a rough sense of scale on the supply side: something over 26,000 net units registered against about 3,000 allocated, so well under one registered unit in five has found a buyer. Treat that as an estimate. It assumes the lowest strategic significance for every site and some habitat rows could not be matched, both of which push the true share lower rather than higher.

    Source: Biodiversity gain sites register, Natural England, complete download analysed 21 August 2026. Open Government Licence v3.0, © Crown copyright. "Area units" are the units that come from habitat measured by area, as opposed to hedgerows and watercourses, which are counted separately.

    Why so many sites sell nothing

    Supply is national. Demand is local.

    This is the single most important idea in the guide, and it is the one that catches people out.

    A developer cannot just buy the cheapest units in England. The metric applies a spatial risk multiplier, which is a discount for distance. If the compensation land is inside the same local planning authority or National Character Area as their development, the units count at full value. In a neighbouring one, they count at 0.75. Anywhere else in England, 0.5.

    Two terms you will meet constantly

    Local planning authority, or LPA. The council that grants planning permission where you are. District, borough, unitary or a national park.

    National Character Area, or NCA. One of the 159 landscape areas Natural England divides England into, such as The Fens or the Shropshire Hills. Your land sits in exactly one, and you can look it up free on the government's MAGIC map.

    Where your land is, relative to the developmentUnits count at
    Same local planning authority or National Character Area1.0
    Neighbouring local planning authority or National Character Area0.75
    Anywhere else in England0.5

    A developer buying from far away has to buy twice as many units for the same result. So they buy local, almost always. Which means your land competes only with the other registered sites near it, for only the development happening near it.

    On top of that there are trading rules. Losses of medium distinctiveness habitat must be replaced either with a habitat in the same broad type, or with anything more distinctive. High distinctiveness must be replaced like for like, and very high needs bespoke compensation agreed with the authority.

    One piece of good news buried in the rules

    The trading rules only bite up to the point of no net loss. Defra's guide: "Once trading rules have been met, biodiversity net gain requirements can be met by the creation and enhancement of any habitat, provided it is within the relevant module."

    So a developer losing grassland must find grassland or better to cover the loss, and can then buy your scrub or your woodland for the 10% gain on top. Your market is wider than a strict like for like reading suggests.

    What you cannot do is cross between modules. Area units, hedgerow units and watercourse units are counted separately and cannot be summed or converted. Hedgerow units only ever compensate hedgerow losses. And note that ponds are area habitats, not watercourse habitats.

    The question that decides everything

    Not "is this good land for nature". It is: how much development is coming in this local planning authority over the next few years, how many registered gain sites are already competing for it, and will my habitat type match what those developers are losing?

    Source: The Statutory Biodiversity Metric User Guide, Defra, June 2026, tables 3 and 9.

    The formula

    How a biodiversity unit is counted

    Ignore the mystique. It is one multiplication, done by a Defra spreadsheet, and you can follow it.

    Units = size in hectares
      × distinctiveness  (how special the habitat type is)
      × condition  (what state it is in)
      × strategic significance  (is it in the right place for local nature plans)

    That gives you the units your land holds today. It is your baseline, and it is the number you want to be low.

    Then for the habitat you are going to create, the same multiplication runs again, with three discounts applied on top:

    New units = hectares × distinctiveness × condition × strategic significance
      × difficulty  (how hard this habitat is to create)
      × time  (how many years until it reaches target condition)

    Your saleable units are the new number minus the baseline number. That difference is the only thing anybody is buying.

    This is why intensively farmed land is the raw material of this market. An arable field scores almost nothing today, so almost everything you create is uplift. A flower rich meadow already scores highly, so there is very little headroom left to sell.

    Distinctiveness

    The government's scoring of habitat types

    Every habitat type in England has a fixed score in the metric. You cannot argue with it.

    BandScoreExamples
    Very high8Lowland meadows, lowland dry acid grassland, upland hay meadows
    High6Lowland mixed deciduous woodland, lowland heathland, traditional orchards, priority ponds
    Medium4Other neutral grassland, mixed scrub, other broadleaved woodland, non priority ponds
    Low2Arable crops, modified (improved) grassland, bracken
    Very low0Sealed surfaces, buildings

    And condition, assessed against Defra's published criteria by an ecologist:

    ConditionScore
    Good3
    Fairly good2.5
    Moderate2
    Fairly poor1.5
    Poor, or not assessed (arable)1

    In practice Defra's published condition assessment sheets return only Good, Moderate or Poor for grassland, scrub, woodland, heathland, wetland, ponds and orchards. Fairly good and fairly poor exist, but only as a one step adjustment an ecologist can justify for site specific features the standard assessment misses. Do not plan around them.

    So a hectare of cereal crops scores 1 × 2 × 1 × 1.0 = 2 units. That is your starting point, and it is a good one.

    Source: Statutory Biodiversity Metric User Guide, June 2026, tables 5 and 6, and the metric calculation tool v1.0.4 sheet "G-1 All Habitats".

    Strategic significance

    The 15% you get for being in the right place

    Strategic significance rewards habitat created where the local nature plan actually wants it. It is a small multiplier with a big effect on the maths, and it is one of the few things you can influence by choosing the right field.

    CategoryMultiplier
    High, formally identified in the local strategy1.15
    Medium, ecologically desirable but not in the strategy1.10
    Low, or no local strategy1.00

    Every area of England is covered by a Local Nature Recovery Strategy, or LNRS. These are being published area by area, and each one comes with a Local Habitat Map showing where specific measures are wanted.

    Two rules that catch people out

    Once your area's LNRS is published, the medium band disappears. You either match the map and get 1.15, or you get 1.0. There is no middle.

    Once it is published, your existing habitat is always given the 1.0 multiplier, never 1.15. That quietly helps you, because it keeps the baseline number you subtract small. Note this is the strategic significance multiplier, nothing to do with the Low distinctiveness band on the previous step.

    To claim 1.15 your parcel must be mapped in the Local Habitat Map as somewhere a measure is proposed, and what you create must be consistent with that mapped measure. Creating woodland where the map asks for grassland gets you nothing extra.

    Source: Statutory Biodiversity Metric User Guide, June 2026, tables 7 and 8.

    The three discounts

    Why the headline number shrinks

    These are the multipliers that turn an exciting number into a realistic one.

    Difficulty

    Set automatically by the metric per habitat. Easy things to create keep their full value. Hard things lose most of it.

    DifficultyMultiplierExamples of creation
    Low1.0Other neutral grassland, mixed scrub, other broadleaved woodland, traditional orchard
    Medium0.67Priority ponds, upland heathland, hazel and willow scrub
    High0.33Lowland meadows, lowland heathland, lowland mixed deciduous woodland, floodplain wetland mosaic
    Very high0.1Coastal sand dunes, limestone pavement

    Time

    Habitat that will not be ready for 20 years is worth less than habitat ready in 5. The metric knocks 3.5% off per year until target condition is reached, using the years Defra has fixed for each habitat and condition.

    Years to target condition5101520253030+
    Value retained84%70%59%49%41%34%32%

    The metric caps the discount at "30 plus" years, worth 32%. Several habitats sit at that cap: lowland mixed deciduous woodland at anything above poor condition, lowland heathland in good condition, other broadleaved woodland in good condition.

    Distance

    The spatial multiplier from the step on why sites do not sell. Applied by the buyer, not by you, but it decides whether they buy from you at all.

    The counter intuitive result

    High distinctiveness habitat sounds like the money maker. It is often not. Lowland mixed deciduous woodland scores 6, but it is high difficulty (0.33) and takes 30 or more years to reach good condition (0.34). Multiply those together and you keep about 11% of what the headline suggests. Other neutral grassland scores only 4, but it is low difficulty (1.0) and reaches moderate condition in 5 years (0.84). You keep 84%.

    That is exactly why the register is dominated by other neutral grassland and mixed scrub, not by ancient style woodland.

    Source: metric calculation tool v1.0.4, sheets "G-3 Multipliers" and "G-4 Temporal multipliers".

    Worked example

    Ten hectares of arable, done two ways

    Same field. Same 10 hectares. Two different habitats. Watch what the discounts do.

    Other neutral grassland, moderate condition

    Baseline: 10 ha × 2 × 1 × 1.0 = 20 units

    After: 10 × 4 × 2 × 1.15 × 1.0 × 0.84 = 77.0 units

    Net uplift: 57 units. At £25,000 a unit that is around £1.43m gross over the agreement, from 10 hectares, if it all sells.

    Lowland mixed deciduous woodland, moderate condition

    Baseline: 10 ha × 2 × 1 × 1.0 = 20 units

    After: 10 × 6 × 2 × 1.15 × 0.33 × 0.34 = 15.5 units

    Net uplift: negative. On the metric, planting that woodland scores fewer units than the arable field it replaced, for 30 years, at a much higher cost.

    Both are good things to do for nature. Only one of them is a business. This is the gap between what feels valuable and what the metric pays for, and it is where most first time enthusiasm goes wrong.

    Now the part that matters more than the £1.43m

    57 units is an enormous quantity to sell into one local planning authority. The median authority in England has absorbed 3.9 units in total since the register opened. Only 7 of 307 have ever taken more than 57. On most authorities that £1.43m is not a payday, it is a sales programme measured in decades, and the costs start in year one either way.

    Before you use a number like this, look up your authority on the register and ask how many units a year it has actually absorbed. Then divide.

    One qualification on the woodland card. Woodland loses here because arable already scores 2 and the discounts are severe. It can still work where the baseline is lower, where a developer losing woodland must replace it like for like and pays the premium those trading rules create, or where woodland creation grants and carbon units are stacked alongside the biodiversity units. It will not work on this arithmetic alone. And on many holdings, improving habitat you already have beats creating it from scratch. On the metric's own tables, enhancing existing grassland up to a lowland meadow carries a difficulty multiplier of 0.67 where creating one from arable carries 0.33, so the enhancement route scores about twice as much. Ask your ecologist to model both.

    The grassland figure assumes you qualify for the 1.15 strategic significance multiplier. Without it the uplift falls to about 47 units. Every assumption matters.

    Show the working

    What this deliberately does not do: apply the buyer's spatial multiplier (that is their calculation, not yours), model inflation or the time value of money, include VAT, include your own time, or include the price of the land. It also assumes your habitat reaches its target condition. It does now handle enhancement as well as creation: where your new habitat is in the same broad group as what is already there, improved pasture to species rich grassland or scrub to heathland, the metric scores it from different tables and the calculator follows them. Sometimes that is worse, because grassland to grassland takes ten years to moderate condition rather than five. Often it is much better, because for lowland meadow, floodplain wetland mosaic and lowland heathland the enhancement difficulty multiplier is 0.67 against 0.33 for creation, so enhancement is worth roughly twice as much. Ask your ecologist to model both. Roughly a third of your cost lands in the first two years while the revenue arrives over decades, so cash flow is a separate and harder question.

    Before you write this number down anywhere

    It ignores tax completely. On HMRC's May 2026 position the revenue is normally taxable trading income at up to 47% including National Insurance, and the timing rarely matches: the money can be taxed in year one while the costs are deducted across 30 years.

    It is not discounted. Money arriving in year 25 is not worth what it is worth today. Treat any large total here as roughly 40% to 45% of its face value in today's money.

    It excludes the land, inflation and VAT. Enter costs net of VAT if you will be registered, since you recover it. Management costs rise every year and this multiplies them flat.

    It is an illustration for thinking with. Not a forecast, not a valuation, not a metric assessment.

    Green flags

    What good land for this looks like

    • A low baseline. Arable, improved grassland, temporary leys. Boring for nature today is the whole point. It means nearly everything you create counts as uplift.
    • Inside a local planning authority with real development pressure. Your buyers are the schemes getting permission within that boundary or National Character Area. No development nearby means no buyers nearby.
    • Few registered gain sites already competing. One 2026 market analysis put registered habitat banks in only about a third of England's local planning authorities. Being the only supplier in a busy authority is worth more than perfect ecology in a quiet one. Check the register for yourself rather than taking anyone's word for it.
    • Mapped in the published Local Nature Recovery Strategy for a measure you can actually deliver. That is your 1.15.
    • Cheap land, but the right kind of cheap. Awkward corners and land you cannot farm hard are good: low baseline, little income given up. Do not assume Grade 4 and 5 is the answer though. Wet, acid, shallow or steep ground pushes you toward habitats with worse difficulty and time multipliers than the neutral grassland the market actually wants, and makes target condition harder to reach. The sweet spot is usually flat, workable, nutrient depleted arable or improved pasture on moderate soils. Boring for nature, easy to convert, quick to reach moderate condition.
    • Something you can create quickly and cheaply. Grassland and scrub keep their value. Long dated, high difficulty habitats do not.
    • Water on or near it, with your eyes open. Watercourse and pond units carry high headline prices because supply is scarce. Demand is scarcer still. Only a developer actually affecting a watercourse can buy watercourse units, and only within a tight catchment. Delivery needs the right ownership and consents, and a pond yields very few units for a great deal of excavation. Treat water as a possible bonus on a site that already works on grassland, never as the reason to buy.
    • Clean title, no tenant, no lender to persuade. Every extra consent is months of delay and a possible veto.

    Red flags

    Land that will waste your money

    • Irreplaceable habitat you would have to damage. Ancient woodland, ancient and veteran trees, blanket bog, limestone pavement, coastal sand dunes, lowland fen and two saltmarsh types are taken out of the metric baseline and cannot be traded. That does not disqualify a site that happens to contain some. Leave it alone and enhancing it can still count towards your units, while the rest of the holding works normally. What kills a deal is needing to clear or drain it to make your numbers work.
    • Recently cleared or degraded ground. Two separate rules bite. For a development site, Schedule 7A of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990 fixes the baseline at what was there before any degrading activity carried out since 30 January 2020 without planning permission, and where the date is disputed it defaults to that day. For a gain site like yours the metric guide is the control: if habitat has been cleared, destroyed or degraded you must use the pre degradation habitat as your baseline, evidence it from imagery and old records, and, this is the sting, assign a higher condition score in the absence of evidence to the contrary. Buying a field that has just been flailed or ploughed can hand you a baseline you cannot beat, judged against you on the precautionary side.
    • Already committed elsewhere. You cannot sell units for something you are already required or already paid to do. Land under an obligation for tree restocking, marine licensing or environmental damage remediation is out. Overlapping agri-environment options need untangling before they count.
    • Uncultivated or semi natural land. Improving it may need an EIA screening decision from Natural England before any work starts. Registering for BNG is not a substitute, and doing the work first can be an offence.
    • Inside a protected site. SSSI, SAC, SPA and Ramsar land needs Natural England's consent before works, and protected species need licences.
    • Already high scoring. A flower rich meadow is wonderful and nearly worthless here, because the headroom above your baseline is what sells.
    • Bought at a BNG premium. If you pay over bare agricultural value because the seller knows the BNG story, the deal has to clear that premium before you earn anything. Sellers read the same articles you do.
    • Land with development hope value. If the parcel has any realistic prospect of getting planning permission one day, that hope is worth far more than biodiversity units, and a 30 year obligation destroys it. Never put land with hope value into this, and if you are buying, check you are not paying for hope you are about to extinguish.
    • Too small to carry the fixed costs. Setup runs to £15,000 or more whatever the size of the parcel, and most operators want somewhere in the region of 8 to 20 hectares before they will look at you. On a three hectare field the fixed costs alone eat a large share of everything you might earn.

    Sources: GOV.UK, Sell biodiversity units as a land manager; GOV.UK, EIA (Agriculture) regulations; Statutory Biodiversity Metric User Guide, June 2026.

    Checking a specific field

    What to look up before you make an offer

    All of it is public, free, and worth an evening of your time before you spend anything. For any parcel you are considering, find out:

    1. Whether it carries a statutory designation. These are the legal protections that come with strings attached: a Site of Special Scientific Interest (SSSI), a Special Area of Conservation (SAC), a Special Protection Area for birds (SPA), a Ramsar wetland, or a national or local nature reserve. Any of them means you need consent before you touch anything.
    2. Whether there is ancient woodland or priority habitat on or beside it.
    3. Its agricultural land classification grade, which tells you both the farming value you would give up and how a planner will view it.
    4. Its flood zone, which shapes what habitat is realistic and what a buyer will believe.
    5. Whether it sits in Green Belt, a National Landscape or a conservation area.
    6. Which Local Nature Recovery Strategy covers it, whether it is published, and what the Local Habitat Map asks for there.
    7. Which local planning authority and National Character Area it falls in, because that is your catchment of buyers.
    8. Which registered gain sites are already nearby, and what habitat they are selling. That is your competition, and it is on the public register.

    The £300 test that de risks the whole thing

    Everything in this guide assumes your habitat reaches its target condition. For species rich grassland created on ex arable, the single best predictor of whether it will is residual soil phosphorus. Years of fertiliser leave P levels that let vigorous grasses smother the wildflowers, and no amount of good seed fixes it quickly.

    A soil test costs a small fraction of one day of anybody's professional fees. Do it before you exchange, not after.

    And the title checks, which no map will show you

    Get these from the seller's solicitor before you make an offer. Any one of them can kill the scheme.

    • Mines and minerals reserved to somebody else, with rights to enter and work them. Common on former estate land. Someone else can lawfully dig up your habitat.
    • Sporting rights reserved to a third party. The holder can enter, drive across it and release birds, and you cannot stop them.
    • Existing restrictive covenants. A covenant not to use the land otherwise than for agriculture, or against planting, is breached the day you take the field out of production. Whoever holds the benefit enforces it, not the council.
    • Rights of way, permissive paths and open access land, which affect what condition the habitat can reach.
    • Easements, wayleaves and pipeline or cable deeds with rights of entry.
    • An existing overage or clawback deed. Entering a BNG agreement can trip a badly drafted trigger and hand a share of your income to the previous owner.
    • An option or promotion agreement in favour of a developer, usually shown as a restriction on the title. They can veto your agreement and they will.

    The shortcut for the map based checks

    Every one of those checks is a separate government map or dataset. GainScout is the app we built to do all of them against a single point on a map in one press, and to show you the source and date for every finding. It is free to download and free to check land with, and you can save a site, add photos and produce a dated report to hand to an agent or ecologist. It is a first look, not a substitute for a survey.

    The official route

    The eight steps, in order

    This is Defra's own sequence for a land manager selling units.

    1. Work out your units. Engage an ecologist for a baseline habitat survey, then use the statutory metric to model what enhancement would produce. Check the local nature recovery strategy while you are at it.
    2. Price them. Your price has to cover creation, 30 years of management, monitoring, professional fees, insurance, machinery and inflation. Price from your costs, not from a headline per unit figure.
    3. Secure the land legally. Either a planning obligation (section 106) with the local planning authority, or a conservation covenant with a designated responsible body. Minimum 30 years, and for a covenant make sure a maximum is written in too. Everyone with an interest must be a party or they are not bound: you, your lender, any tenant, any option holder, anyone with reserved rights. The council is not obliged to enter one, so confirm they will before you spend anything. At the same time, agree a habitat management and monitoring plan.
    4. Find a buyer. Marketplaces, land agents, direct developer relationships, the council's own signposting, or a habitat bank operator. There is no guaranteed buyer.
    5. Do the deal. Number and type of units, commitment to allocate, price structure (lump sum, staged, or results based) and payment timing.
    6. Register the gain site with Natural England. £639 per site, payable within 28 days. You need proof of ownership, a boundary map, the signed legal agreement, the completed metric calculation, the habitat management and monitoring plan, and a local land charge search certificate dated within the last two months. They tell you within six weeks whether it is accepted, rejected, or needs more from you. Once decided you cannot withdraw and the fee is not refunded.
    7. Record the allocation of specific units to a specific development on the register. This matters more than it sounds. The biodiversity gain condition is attached to the planning permission, and the developer cannot lawfully begin the development until the council approves their biodiversity gain plan, which needs your allocation in place. That is your leverage on payment timing. Get paid on allocation, not afterwards.
    8. Manage it for 30 years from completion of the works, reporting to the council or responsible body. If work has not started when you allocate, you must begin within 12 months. Note that the 30 years runs from completion of the creation works, not from the day you buy. Counting from exchange, the real commitment is closer to 32 or 35 years.

    Steps 3, 6 and 7 can happen before, during or after finding a buyer. That flexibility is what makes both habitat banking and bespoke creation possible.

    Source: GOV.UK, Sell biodiversity units as a land manager. Checked 7 September 2026.

    Timing

    How long it really takes

    StageTypical time
    Baseline habitat survey1 to 2 months, and only in season
    Habitat management and monitoring plan2 to 3 months
    Legal agreement (section 106 or covenant)2 to 3 months
    Registration with Natural England1 to 2 months, six week target
    Land assessment through to first unit sale12 to 24 months

    The survey window is a hard deadline, not a preference

    Botanical surveys have to be done when the sward is identifiable. April to September is the outer envelope, and for the grassland condition assessment your baseline actually turns on it is realistically May to August. A hay cut or hard grazing inside that window can spoil it too.

    An out of season survey is not automatically void, since Defra asks for the optimum time of year and a precautionary approach where evidence is thin. But it weakens your baseline, and Natural England at registration, or the council approving your management plan, can send you back for another season.

    Practically: if you complete on a purchase in October, your baseline survey is waiting until spring. That is roughly six months of holding costs before the clock even starts. Buy in the winter and budget for it.

    The other survey seasons, and the consents nobody costs

    If protected species are on the land, their survey windows are narrower than the botanical one and missing them costs a year. Great crested newt eDNA runs mid April to the end of June. Bat activity surveys May to September. Breeding birds March to July. Reptiles April to October. You may also need a licence for the habitat works themselves, not just for development: scrub clearance, pond creation and re profiling can all need one.

    Two more that catch people out. Planting woodland above the threshold needs a separate Forestry Commission EIA screening, and felling later needs a licence carrying restocking conditions. Pond, wetland and floodplain works commonly need a flood risk activity permit, land drainage consent or an abstraction licence from the Environment Agency.

    Then add the part nobody schedules: waiting for a buyer. That is not a stage with a duration. It is a market, and for four in ten registered sites it has not happened yet.

    Sources: habitat bank setup timelines, 2025 to 2026 industry guidance; CIEEM BNG technical guide on survey seasonality.

    The number that matters

    You need roughly £20,000 to £40,000 in the first two years, on top of whatever the land cost, before a single unit has sold. Then £1,000 to £3,000 every year for three decades.

    Income, if it comes, starts somewhere between one and three years in and arrives in fragments. Lenders do not readily lend against unsold biodiversity units, so this is a working capital problem before it is a return problem.

    If losing that first two years of money would genuinely hurt you, do not do this on your own. Use an operator, who carries it, or do not do it.

    If you are VAT registered you recover the VAT, so the plus VAT amounts are timing rather than cost. If you are not, add up to 20%.

    The bill that keeps arriving

    The 30 year liability is the part people underestimate most, because it is the part that has not happened yet.

    Put the other way round, 30 years of management and monitoring costs roughly £1,500 to £4,500 per biodiversity unit in today's money, depending on habitat. Any price you are quoted per unit should be read against that. A £25,000 unit that costs you £4,500 to service is a very different proposition from one that costs £1,500.

    And that is before inflation

    Those are today's costs. The bill arrives every year for 30 years while your unit price was fixed on day one. At 2.5% inflation, £400 per hectare this year is about £840 per hectare in year 30, and the cash you actually have to find across the term is roughly 45% more than a flat multiplication suggests. If your costs are going to be index linked, your income needs to be too. Ask for that in the contract.

    And if the habitat does not get there

    If monitoring shows the habitat is not reaching its target condition, you take the corrective action set out in your plan. Reseeding, replanting, changed grazing, drainage work. That cost is yours, and the units are already sold.

    Three ways in

    Do it yourself, or let somebody else

    You do not have to run this alone, and for most first time buyers running it alone is the wrong call. The trade is simple: the less risk you carry, the less you keep.

    RouteYou payYou get
    Sell independently £15,000 to £20,000 + VAT setup, plus creation, plus 30 years of management and monitoring, plus £25,000 or more of monitoring across the term All of the unit revenue if it sells, and all of the risk if it does not. About £143,000 per hectare of gross revenue on the neutral grassland example, at 5.7 units per hectare and £25,000 a unit, spread across the agreement and only if every unit sells. Take off commission, 30 years of management and monitoring and tax and, on a full sell through, you keep roughly £60,000 to £80,000 per hectare across 30 years. At the register's median sell through you keep nothing and pay to manage the land
    Habitat bank operator Nothing to about £5,000 + VAT, often refunded when units sell. You lease the land to them Published examples: around £240 per ha per year lease plus up to £660 per ha per year management, index linked, a welcome payment up to £10,000 and a 50:50 share of upside. Another operator quotes £222 to £297 per ha per year guaranteed rent plus creation, management and contingency paid at cost, plus 50:50 on surplus
    Group or partnership Shared setup with neighbours or a conservation body A bigger, more saleable block than any of you has alone. You need a written agreement on who does what and who is paid what

    Compare net against net, never gross against net

    The independent figure above is gross revenue. The operator figures are income. They are not comparable as written, and the difference flatters the risky route. One operator's number bundles reimbursement of costs you then spend, the other does not.

    Ask any operator for the number that lands in your account after everything you are responsible for, per hectare, per year, index linked, in writing, and work out the same figure for yourself before you choose.

    What share of the money does an operator keep?

    A great deal, and it is rarely stated as a percentage. One chartered surveyor's analysis of a lease based operator deal put the landowner's share of gross sale proceeds at 4.3%, against 90% to 95% for a landowner selling their own units through an independent agent and paying professional fees.

    That is not automatically a bad deal. The operator is funding the creation, carrying 30 years of cost and taking the risk that nothing sells, which on the register evidence is a real risk with real money behind it. But you should know the split before you sign, not after. Ask what percentage of gross unit revenue you receive, and ask to see it worked through on your own acreage.

    The operator terms above are figures those businesses published in 2025 and 2026. We have not verified them, they are not offers, and terms change.

    Source: Biodiversity Net Gain opportunities for farmers, enterprise budget, comparing published operator terms; GOV.UK on the three operating models.

    What you sign

    Section 106 or conservation covenant

    You must legally secure the habitat for at least 30 years before you can register. There are two instruments and they are not equivalent.

    Planning obligation, section 106

    Made with the local planning authority where your land sits, not the developer's. Familiar to every rural solicitor, cheaper to draft, and the council enforces it.

    Check it is actually available before you spend anything. A council is not obliged to enter one, and some decline agreements for a gain site with no development attached, or charge heavily for them.

    Conservation covenant

    Made with a designated responsible body. Only the Secretary of State is one automatically. Every other body, councils included, has to apply to Defra and be designated. As at May 2026 there were 48, and roughly 20 of those are private companies rather than charities or public bodies. Check which kind you are dealing with.

    More flexible and often faster to agree, but legal costs are higher. If a designated body later loses its designation, section 132 of the Environment Act 2021 moves its covenants to the Secretary of State, so the agreement does not simply lapse.

    The trap in a conservation covenant: it lasts for ever by default

    Section 121 of the Environment Act 2021 sets the default period for freehold land as "a period of indefinite duration", unless the covenant provides for a shorter one. Biodiversity Net Gain only needs 30 years.

    So if the deed is silent on when it ends, you have encumbered your freehold permanently in exchange for a 30 year payment. Make sure a defined term is written in, and that the land is released at the end of it. A section 106 is normally drafted with an express term, which is one real advantage of that route.

    Alongside either, you agree a habitat management and monitoring plan. That document, not the sales brochure, is what you are committing to do every year for three decades.

    It is a local land charge, not a note on your deeds

    Both instruments bind whoever buys or inherits the land, but only if registered in the local land charges register before they acquire it. A buyer finds it through a local search, not through a Land Registry search. That is why Natural England asks for a local land charge search certificate dated within the last two months when you register.

    Sources: Environment Act 2021 s.121 and s.122; TCPA 1990 s.106; designated responsible bodies.

    Getting out

    Can you ever end it?

    Short answer: assume not, and price it that way. The detail differs by instrument and it is worth knowing before you choose.

    Section 106

    You can apply to the council to modify or discharge it, but not for five years (section 106A, Town and Country Planning Act 1990), with an appeal to the Secretary of State if refused.

    That route is largely shut for this purpose. Since February 2024 a council may not discharge or modify an obligation where doing so would prevent a development meeting its biodiversity gain objective, or create a significant risk of that. Once your units are allocated, the door closes.

    Conservation covenant

    You can only vary or end it by agreement with the responsible body (sections 127 to 129, Environment Act 2021). If they say no, your only route is an application to the Upper Tribunal, Lands Chamber, under section 130.

    Section 84 of the Law of Property Act 1925, the familiar route for lifting an old restrictive covenant, is expressly disapplied.

    What does release you, and what does not

    Selling the land. For a conservation covenant, section 122 of the Environment Act 2021 releases you from future performance once you stop holding the estate. You stay liable for breaches that happened on your watch. For a section 106, section 106(4) allows the deed to release you on the same basis, but only if that clause is actually in it. Ask your solicitor to confirm which release you have before you sign.

    What does not release you: changing your mind, the market falling, the developer failing, or the habitat proving harder than expected.

    Sources: TCPA 1990 s.106A; Environment Act 2021 Part 7.

    Who else has to agree

    Lenders, tenants and the people after you

    Your mortgage lender, and the priority problem

    If the land is charged you need written consent, for either instrument, and you need more than consent. The lender should be joined as a party and postpone its charge.

    Here is why. A charge that came first ranks ahead of your agreement. A lender enforcing its security could sell the land free of the obligation, wiping out every unit sold from the site and leaving you facing the buyers. No developer's solicitor will accept units secured behind a prior charge. Entering the agreement without consent is also likely to breach your mortgage conditions outright. Ask the lender before you exchange on the land, and budget for their fees and their solicitor's.

    Any tenant

    If the land is let, you cannot secure it for 30 years on your own. A 1986 Act agricultural tenancy carries statutory succession rights on death and on retirement, so it can outlast you by two generations. A farm business tenancy binds you for its term. Many arrangements called "grazing licences" are tenancies in law whatever the paper says.

    And the tenant, not you, is the person in occupation who would actually have to carry out the management plan. They must be a party, and they will want paying. Find out exactly what is let, on what terms, and when it ends, before you buy. A tenancy can make a field completely unusable for this.

    If you die during the 30 years

    The agreement does not die with you. It stays attached to the land and passes to whoever inherits, along with the obligation to keep managing it and pay for it. Your executors cannot distribute or sell the land free of it. They can sell, but the next owner takes the same obligation, and some buyers will discount heavily for it.

    If leaving the farm clean to your children matters more to you than the income, that is a perfectly good reason not to do this. Whatever you decide, tell whoever will administer your estate that this exists and where the deed is.

    If you go bankrupt

    The obligation attaches to the land, not only to you. Insolvency can end your personal liability, but the land stays burdened and whoever takes it takes it subject to the agreement. In a forced sale a burdened site is worth less than clean land, and your unit buyers are left as unsecured creditors.

    Sources: legal commentary from rural practices including Foot Anstey and Shakespeare Martineau; Environment Act 2021 s.122; Agricultural Holdings Act 1986.

    What it does to the value of the land

    The trap that ruins people who do everything else right

    A valuer looking at your parcel after the agreement is signed will value it twice and take the higher figure: as restricted bare land that can no longer be farmed commercially, or as an investment, capitalising the remaining contracted income with a reversion.

    That second approach only helps if income is still to come.

    Sell every unit up front and you can end up owning a liability

    If you have sold all your units and spent the proceeds, there is no income left to capitalise. What remains is land that carries 29 more years of management cost with nothing coming in against it. On a valuation that parcel can be worth nil, or less than nil. A buyer would need paying to take it.

    Two ways to avoid it. Keep a tail of unsold units so there is future income attached to the land. Or ring fence a sinking fund out of the first sales, sized to cover the remaining management, and do not touch it.

    Expect the encumbered parcel to be worth materially less than unencumbered arable in any case. The buyer pool is small and the buyer inherits the bill. If you have borrowed against the land, check what the post agreement value does to your loan to value covenant, and consider getting a formal valuation before and after.

    Risks, part one

    The four that cost people the most

    1. You never find a buyer

    The biggest one, and the least discussed. Nearly four in ten registered sites had sold nothing. The median site has sold under one unit, and the median local planning authority has absorbed under four. There is no obligation on anyone to buy.

    The part people miss is what happens next. Once you have signed the section 106 or the covenant, you owe 30 years of management and monitoring whether or not a single unit ever sells. On the figures in the calculator that is well over £100,000 on a ten hectare site, paid out across three decades against no income at all. Not £20,000 of wasted fees. A six figure commitment.

    The way to avoid it is simple and almost nobody says it out loud: do not sign the legal agreement until you have a buyer in writing. The official guidance lets you register and secure the land before, during or after finding a buyer. Nothing forces you to commit first. Anyone urging you to get secured and registered now, and worry about buyers later, is describing their timetable and not yours.

    2. The price moves against you

    Prices have already softened for the commonest habitats. Other neutral grassland units fell around 6% in the north and 3% in the south in the four months to February 2026, and traditional orchard units fell 16% and 10%. Meanwhile supply keeps arriving. You are pricing a 30 year commitment against a market with three years of history.

    3. The habitat does not perform

    Wrong soil, wrong weather, wrong seed, deer, drought, a bad first winter. If monitoring says you missed target condition, the remedial work is yours, at your cost, after the units have been sold and the money spent.

    4. Costs run past the revenue

    Thirty years of management, seven or eight monitoring reports, monitoring fees, inflation. Sell your units too cheap in year one and you have signed up to 29 more years of paying for it. This is the trap the government guidance is warning about when it says price from your costs.

    Price movement source: Biodiversity Units UK, February 2026 pricing report, 165 habitat banks surveyed.

    Risks, part two

    The four people do not see coming

    5. The rules change under you

    They already have. On 6 August 2026 the government exempted developments of 0.2 hectares or less, exempted temporary permissions of five years or less, and removed the self build exemption. Defra's own estimate is that the 0.2 hectare change alone removes BNG from about half of all residential planning permissions. Ecologists warned it risked collapsing the market. A brownfield exemption was consulted on in 2026, with professional bodies warning the combined exemptions could take up to 90% of applications out of scope.

    Read that against the register. 78% of all recorded allocations were for less than one unit, which is small builders buying fractions. Small sites are roughly half of all demand for off site units. So the August 2026 exemption does not merely thin the pipeline, it removes the exact customer most registered sites have been living on. If your plan is fractional sales to local housebuilders, that plan was written for a market that changed on 6 August 2026. And your 30 year commitment has no exit if demand falls.

    6. The person who owes you money fails

    If a developer becomes insolvent after you have created the habitat but before they have paid in full, your obligation to the council or responsible body generally continues anyway. You can end up holding a 30 year liability against a debt you cannot collect.

    7. Nobody is regulating this

    Unit sales are unregulated. Brokers, agents and habitat bank operators are not licensed for this. There is no ombudsman and no compensation scheme. Your protection is the contract you sign and the solicitor who reads it.

    8. Enforcement is uneven

    Which sounds like it favours you, and does not. It means the value of everyone's units depends on a system whose credibility is unproven, and it means a determined responsible body can pursue you for a breach 20 years from now while a stretched council might not pursue the site next door at all.

    Sources: Defra, BNG amendments and transitional arrangements; CIEEM.

    Tax, part one

    Income or capital, and why it is worse than 45%

    This changed recently and it changes the arithmetic. HMRC published a technical note on the taxation of ecosystem service payments on 14 May 2026.

    • Income, not capital, as the starting point. HMRC's position is that payments for ecosystem services, including biodiversity units, are usually taxed as income, and normally as income of a trade. You are treated as creating and selling units, not as disposing of land.
    • It is worse than the 45% headline. On trading income an individual also pays Class 4 National Insurance, 2% above £50,270, so the top rate is effectively 47%. A large lump sum also drags income through the £100,000 to £125,140 band where the personal allowance is withdrawn and the effective marginal rate is 60%. Capital gains tax, by contrast, tops out at 24%.
    • Capital treatment is the exception, and 30 years does not buy it. It applies where the payment genuinely compensates for permanent loss of use or sterilisation of the asset. HMRC is explicit that the length of the agreement is only one factor and does not on its own decide the matter.
    • If it is capital, it is a part disposal. A capital sum derived from land you keep is taxed under section 22 of the Taxation of Chargeable Gains Act 1992, with only a slice of your original purchase price set against it. On land bought cheaply there is very little base cost, so most of the receipt is taxed.
    • If it is a farming trade, you can average it. HMRC confirms the farming rules, including farmers' averaging over two or five years, apply to farming trades that include ecosystem services income. On a lump sum that can save a great deal, and it is the main practical reason to keep farming the rest of the holding.
    • Commercially occupied woodland sits outside income tax entirely. Section 11 of ITTOIA 2005 says commercial occupation of woodlands is not a trade for income tax, and HMRC confirms units created on that land fall outside a trade too. The expenditure falls outside as well. There is no equivalent for peatland.

    The trap that catches most people: taxed in year one, spending for 30 years

    You may receive most of the money at the start and spend it over three decades. Tax does not automatically follow that shape.

    On the cash basis, which has been the default for unincorporated businesses since 2024/25 and so may be you without you ever choosing it, HMRC says the receipt is taxed in full when you receive it. No relief for the 29 years of management still to come until you actually spend it.

    On the accruals basis, HMRC says the income follows accounting standards, which may require it to be recognised up front and or spread over the life of the agreement. Getting it spread and matched against the cost is usually the right answer, but it depends on how the contract is written, not on what you would prefer afterwards.

    You generally cannot make a provision now for future habitat management and deduct it. Get an accountant onto the basis of accounting and the revenue recognition before you sign the unit sale contract.

    Source: HMRC technical note on ecosystem services, 14 May 2026; Stephens Scown analysis.

    Tax, part two

    VAT, inheritance, and who should own it

    VAT, and registration is not optional

    • Unit sales by a taxable person are standard rated at 20%. You must register once taxable turnover passes £90,000 in any rolling 12 months, and a single unit sale can do it on its own.
    • Always contract "plus VAT". Agree a bare figure and you have agreed a VAT inclusive price, and you keep a sixth less than you thought.
    • The upside: once registered, VAT on your setup costs, professional fees and 30 years of management is recoverable. So the "plus VAT" figures in the cost tables are a cash flow item for you, not a real cost.
    • If you lease the land to an operator rather than selling units yourself, that lease is VAT exempt unless you opt to tax, and without the option you may lose recovery on everything you spent getting there. Decide before you grant the lease.
    • The government's statutory credits are outside the scope of VAT, so their published prices are not directly comparable with a VAT inclusive private price.

    Inheritance tax

    • Since 6 April 2025, land in a qualifying environmental management agreement can keep agricultural property relief.
    • From 6 April 2026, agricultural and business property relief together are limited to a £2.5m allowance per person at 100% relief, with 50% relief above that, an effective inheritance tax rate of 20% rather than 40%, payable in interest free instalments over ten years. The allowance was originally announced at £1m and raised to £2.5m in December 2025. Any unused part is transferable to a surviving spouse or civil partner, so a couple can cover up to £5m.
    • Three conditions the headline hides. The land must have been agricultural property for the two years before the agreement started, so you cannot buy a field and covenant it the same month. The agreement must be with government, a public body, a local authority or an approved responsible body, which a section 106 or conservation covenant is and a private contract with an unregulated operator is not. And the usual test still applies: two years if you farm it yourself, seven if somebody else occupies it, which is what leasing to an operator does.
    • Business property relief can be lost by leasing. Relief is denied where a business consists wholly or mainly of holding investments. A site that has sold its units and collects rent looks like an investment business. The operator lease route is the version most at risk. Land inside a wider farming trade is in a much stronger position.

    Who should own it

    An individual pays up to 45% plus 2% Class 4 National Insurance, with a 60% band on the way through. A partnership taxes each partner on their share as it arises, so a lump sum hits every partner's marginal rate in the same year including partners who saw none of the cash. A company pays corporation tax at 19% up to £50,000 and 25% above £250,000, which caps the rate well below 47%, but you pay again to get the money out. Decide before you buy the land, because moving it afterwards is a disposal.

    And the purchase itself

    Bare farmland is non residential for stamp duty land tax. Ask whether the seller has opted to tax: if they have, VAT is added to the price and SDLT is then charged on the VAT inclusive figure. If you are going to register for VAT, do it before you complete, not after.

    Sources: GOV.UK on the changes to agricultural property relief; IHTA 1984 s.124C; SDLT non residential rates; HMRC technical note as above.

    Just changed

    What happened on 6 August 2026

    If you read anything about BNG written before August 2026, check it against this.

    • A new 0.2 hectare exemption. Developments with a red line boundary of 0.2 hectares or less are out of mandatory BNG, unless on site priority habitat is harmed. The red line boundary is the outline drawn round the site on the planning application, and it covers the whole thing, access roads and gardens included.
    • Temporary development exempted. Permissions of five years or less where the land is reinstated afterwards.
    • The hierarchy relaxed for minor development. Minor schemes still in scope can now go straight to buying registered off site units, without first having to show they explored on site delivery. Good news for sellers of registered units.
    • The self build exemption removed. Self and custom build now follow the standard rules, though many will qualify for the new small site exemption anyway.
    • Transitional rule. All of it applies only to applications submitted on or after 6 August 2026. Earlier applications carry on under the old rules whatever their size.

    Source: Defra environment blog, 14 July 2026.

    Coming next

    What to watch

    Big infrastructure joins, 2 November 2026

    BNG becomes mandatory for nationally significant infrastructure projects: major transport, energy and water schemes. That is genuine new demand and the most significant on the horizon. Be careful what it means for you, though. Schemes that size buy through framework deals with large operators, deliver a great deal on their own extensive landholdings, and can fall back on statutory credits. A single small registered site is unlikely to be their counterparty directly. The realistic route into that demand is through an operator, or a group of neighbouring landowners offering a block with some scale.

    A metric change to spatial risk

    Defra has announced that spatial risk for area habitats will be assessed using Local Nature Recovery Strategy areas only, instead of local planning authority and National Character Area boundaries. That redraws the map of who can buy from you. As of September 2026 the published metric guide still uses the old boundaries, with updated tools expected later in 2026.

    This is the same multiplier this guide called the single most important idea in it, and the government has already said it is changing. If you are buying land whose whole case rests on which side of an authority boundary it sits, the boundary that will matter is not the one on today's map. Check the draft LNRS areas before you exchange. If the parcel only works under the current boundaries, do not buy it.

    A brownfield residential exemption

    Consulted on during 2026. If it lands on top of the existing exemptions, professional bodies estimate up to 90% of planning applications could fall outside BNG.

    Stacking with nutrient credits

    Government guidance confirms you can sell both biodiversity units and nutrient neutrality credits from the same land, provided you are not paid twice for the same outcome. In the right catchment that is a second income stream. Note that nutrient agreements typically run far longer than 30 years.

    Sources: Defra, what is changing; GOV.UK on combining environmental payments.

    The comparison nobody offers you

    What else that land could be doing

    Every pitch you will hear compares Biodiversity Net Gain against nothing. A decision needs two columns. Here is the other one, per hectare per year, for land of the kind that suits this.

    What you could do with itRoughly, per hectare per yearCommitment
    Let it for grazing£200 to £350Season by season, or a short tenancy
    Let it as arable, bare land£220 to £865, average around £230A farm business tenancy, usually a few years
    Sustainable Farming Incentive and Countryside StewardshipVaries by action, the highest now above £600Typically 3 to 5 years, capped per agreement, and application windows

    Two honest observations from that table. The operator route is in the same range as simply letting good arable land, for a commitment ten times longer. And the independent route only beats the alternatives if you sell a decent share of your units, which most registered sites have not.

    You can also do more than one

    Government guidance confirms you can stack biodiversity units with nutrient neutrality credits, and with agri-environment schemes, provided you are not paid twice for the same outcome on the same ground. Different interventions on different parcels of the same holding is normal good practice. Ask an adviser to map which of your fields does which job.

    And one thing nobody mentions: year 31. The agreement ends and the land does not have to stay in biodiversity use, but that does not mean everything reverts. Stacked agreements can run longer than the BNG one. Designations can accrue over three decades. Protected species that arrive because you made good habitat bring constraints that outlive the covenant. And woodland you plant is effectively permanent, because felling licences carry restocking conditions. If you plant trees, you are not making a 30 year decision.

    Sources: agricultural rent ranges from 2026 market commentary and Defra farm business tenancy data; SFI 2026 scheme information; GOV.UK on combining environmental payments.

    Before you buy the land

    The checklist

    Work through this before you make an offer, not after.

    1. Ask the seller's solicitor for the title register, title plan and every document referred to in it, and read them for reserved minerals, reserved sporting rights, restrictive covenants limiting use to agriculture, existing overage, and any option or promotion agreement in a developer's favour.
    2. Run the public data checks from the "what to look up" step on the exact parcel.
    3. Identify the local planning authority and National Character Area, and get a feel for how much development is actually coming there.
    4. Search the biodiversity gain sites register for competing sites nearby, and note what habitat they sell.
    5. Find out whether the Local Nature Recovery Strategy is published and what its habitat map asks for on that parcel.
    6. Get an ecologist's opinion on baseline and realistic uplift before exchange, even a short one. This is the number the whole deal rests on.
    7. Confirm nothing disqualifies it: irreplaceable habitat, degradation since 30 January 2020, existing obligations, EIA screening needed.
    8. Price the full 30 year cost, not just the setup.
    9. Speak to your lender and check any tenancy.
    10. Speak to an accountant before you agree the payment structure, not after. The agenda: income or capital; whether the receipt falls inside your farming trade so that farmers' averaging is available; cash basis or accruals, and whether the income can be spread over the term rather than taxed in year one; VAT registration and always quoting plus VAT; Class 4 National Insurance; whether an individual, a partnership or a company should own it; and whether agricultural and business property relief survive the structure, particularly if you are leasing to an operator.
    11. Instruct a solicitor who has done a BNG agreement before. This is not general conveyancing.
    12. Decide honestly whether you can afford to spend the setup money and sell nothing. If you cannot, use an operator or do not do it.
    13. Buy conditionally. Make completion conditional on a satisfactory baseline survey and metric result, on your lender's written consent, and on the council or responsible body confirming they will enter the agreement. If the seller will not accept conditions, that tells you something. Never complete first and survey afterwards, because the survey window is April to September and you cannot undo a purchase.
    14. Make the land purchase stack up on its own terms. If the deal only works because of BNG, you are taking a market risk on a market with three years of history.
    Before you sign with anyone

    Questions for a broker, operator or buyer

    Ask every one of these in writing. How they answer tells you as much as what they answer.

    • What lands in my bank account, per hectare per year, after all costs I am responsible for, and is it index linked?
    • Who pays for remedial work if the habitat misses target condition, and is that written down?
    • What happens if you go out of business? Who takes over the obligations, and how is that secured?
    • Are payments guaranteed or contingent on units selling? If contingent, what happens if nothing sells?
    • How many units on your other sites are still unsold, and how long have they been on the register?
    • Which local planning authority are we selling into, and what evidence do you have of demand there?
    • Who holds the legal agreement, and have you asked my lender for consent?
    • What is the total 30 year cost in your model, and what inflation assumption is in it?
    • Can I exit? Under what terms, and at what cost?
    • Can you show me a completed deal on the public register that I can look up myself?
    • What percentage of gross unit revenue do I actually receive, worked through on my acreage?
    • Are you acting for me or the buyer, and are you paid by both?
    • Is there a bond, escrow or parent company guarantee behind your payment and management promises, who holds it, and is it index linked?
    • Are you regulated, and what professional indemnity cover do you carry?
    • Show me the draft agreement and the management plan before I sign heads of terms, not after.
    • Have you obtained my lender's consent and dealt with priority against the charge, and who pays for that?
    • Can I still claim SFI or Countryside Stewardship on this land, and who keeps those payments?
    • What am I signing in terms of exclusivity or lock out, and for how long?
    • Give me two landowner references who have completed and been paid.

    If you take one thing from this guide

    Every credible route through this starts with the same two people: an ecologist who will give you a real baseline, and a solicitor who has done one of these before. Neither is expensive next to the cost of getting this wrong for 30 years.

    The honest summary

    If you remember five things

    1. Your buyer is local. The whole business is decided by how much development is coming to your local planning authority and how many people are already selling into it. Ecology comes second.
    2. Boring land is good land. A low baseline is the asset. Fast, easy habitat beats rare, difficult habitat on the metric almost every time.
    3. The median registered site has sold under one unit. The good outcomes are real and they are not typical. Plan for the median, not the brochure.
    4. You are signing a 30 year liability, not making a sale. It runs with the land, it needs your lender's blessing, and the remedial work is yours.
    5. Do not let the land purchase depend on it. If the field only makes sense because of BNG, you are taking a market risk on a market younger than most mortgages.

    Done carefully, on the right land, in the right place, this is a genuine and worthwhile income from land that was not earning much. Done on a brochure and a hunch, it is an expensive way to give away three decades of control over a field.
